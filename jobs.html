05 — Part-Time Jobs

Working while you study

International students can work part-time in Germany, but the rules differ by nationality, and going over the limit can put your residence permit at risk. Here's what's allowed, and where to look.

06 — Working-hour limits

How much you're allowed to work

Student statusLimit during semesterNotes
Non-EU / non-EEA students140 full days or 280 half days per yearExceeding this can jeopardise your residence permit renewal — track your days carefully
EU / EEA / Swiss studentsNo fixed day limitSame working rights as German students, though study progress is still expected
On-campus student assistant (HiWi) rolesOften exempt from the day-limit capConfirm current rules with your university's International Office and the Ausländerbehörde

Confirm before you accept extra hours

Day-limit rules and exemptions can change, and your specific visa conditions matter more than general guidance. Check your residence permit and ask your university's International Office before taking on a second job or extra shifts.

07 — Job types

Where students typically work

Minijob

A low-hours job under a set monthly earnings threshold, common in retail, hospitality and delivery. Minijobs are generally exempt from most employee social security contributions, which is why they're a popular first job for students.

Working student (Werkstudent)

A part-time role — typically up to 20 hours a week during term — often in an office or technical field related to your studies. Werkstudent roles usually carry a reduced social security contribution, and many turn into full-time offers after graduation.

On-campus roles

Universities hire student assistants (Hilfskraft/HiWi) for research, library, tutoring and administrative work. These are convenient, flexible around your timetable, and look good on a German CV.

Freelance (Werkvertrag)

Less common for first jobs — freelancing involves separate tax registration and isn't a straightforward fallback if you're unfamiliar with German tax filing. Get advice before taking freelance work as a student.

08 — Where to search

Job platforms worth checking

Your university's Career Service and International Office often post student-specific openings before they reach public job boards. Beyond that, general platforms like StepStone and Indeed list Werkstudent and Minijob roles, and LinkedIn is increasingly used for student positions at larger companies. Notice boards (physical and online) at your Mensa and library are still a reliable source for local, informal work.

09 — Tax & insurance

How work affects your paperwork

Give your employer your Tax ID

You cannot be paid correctly without the Steuer-ID from the Documentation stage — have it ready before your first day.

Understand the "student privilege"

Working under 20 hours a week during term as a registered student typically exempts you from most statutory pension, unemployment and long-term care contributions — but you'll still pay into health insurance. Rules shift at higher hours or during semester breaks, so don't assume the exemption applies automatically.

File a tax return if you're owed a refund

Students who paid income tax during the year can often reclaim some of it by filing a Steuererklärung after the tax year ends. It's optional for many students, but frequently worthwhile.
